> Goal: a packet capture of a staged intrusion. Every packet carries a short base64 payload, the flag left one slice at a time, and the frames are not stored in the order they were sent.

## Approach

The download is a `.pcap`, so this is Wireshark rather than a shell. The artefact link is generated per instance; copy the one on your own challenge page rather than the one below.

```bash frame="code" title="Bash"
┌──(Idan@Kali)-[~/Ph4nt0m_1ntrud3r]
└─> wget https://challenge-files.picoctf.net/c_verbal_sleep/a917f567b9cc0f1a730a7801b309955df4d2234a8114326857b9759e9e5d0453/myNetworkTraffic.pcap
┌──(Idan@Kali)-[~/Ph4nt0m_1ntrud3r]
└─> wireshark myNetworkTraffic.pcap
```

The capture opens in frame order, which is file order. The timestamps in this file do not climb with the frame numbers, which is the entire challenge. Clicking the Time column header sorts the display by timestamp, and only the display, so the frame numbers stay where they are and the file on disk is untouched.

### Reading the payloads

Sorted by time, the traffic separates into a long stretch of short payloads and then a run of longer ones at the end. Select one of those, expand the TCP layer, right click the payload field and choose Show Packet Bytes, then set Decode as to Base64 and Show as to ASCII. The later packets come back as clean readable text, seven characters at a time. Everything before them decodes to bytes that are not text at all, which is what the capture is padded with.

Length is a good first look and a poor filter. Six of the seven slices are seven characters, which base64 stretches to twelve, but the last slice is the closing brace on its own and encodes to four, making the packet that carries the end of the flag the shortest in the whole capture, four characters where the padding is eight. What actually separates signal from padding is that one decodes to readable ASCII and the other does not. Read the run top to bottom in the sorted order and the slices concatenate.

The eight hexadecimal characters near the end are minted per instance, so the fifth and sixth slices are different for everyone and the other five, the closing brace included, are the same.

## Why it works

Slicing a secret across packets defeats anything inspecting one packet at a time, and base64 defeats anything grepping for the literal string, but neither survives reassembly, and reassembly is what a capture is for. The ordering is the only step that costs anything, and even that announces itself, because a reader who trusts frame numbers reassembles the right pieces in the wrong sequence and gets a visible scramble rather than a plausible wrong answer.

Frame numbers are assigned as the file is read, so they record the order somebody wrote the packets down, not the order the packets happened. Captures that are out of order in that sense are ordinary, which is why Wireshark presents the file as it stands and leaves the sorting to whoever is reading it.
